## 1. The clock and the exponent

**1.1 [PROVED].** At size `j`, the minimum of the `j` independent rate-one exponential residual waiting times is exponential of rate `j`, independent of the uniform minimizing vertex. That vertex's mark chooses a uniform neighbour. Restarting clocks at each jump is valid by the memorylessness of the exponential law and independence of the new clock and marks. Thus the holding times are independent `Exp(j)` variables and the jump chain has the stated law. From size `m`, the hitting time `tau_j` of size `j` is `sum_{i=m}^{j-1} E_i/i`, with `E_i` independent unit exponentials.

**1.2 [STANDARD].** By the convergence theorem for independent centered summands with summable variances (the variances here are `1/i^2`), `sum_{i=m}^{j-1}(E_i-1)/i` converges almost surely. Since `sum_{i=m}^{j-1}1/i-log j` has a finite limit (by the integral comparison with error summable as `O(i^{-2})`), `tau_j-log j` has a finite limit. There is no explosion. Also `log n(t)-t -> -lim_j(tau_j-log j)`, by squeezing between `log j-tau_j` and `log j-tau_{j+1}` during the holding interval at size `j`. In particular `e^{-t}n(t) -> Z`, with `0<Z<infty` almost surely.

**1.3 [PROVED].** The size process groups births into families according to the initial ancestor of the *ringing vertex*, and the newborn uses an independent clock in that family irrespective of its attachment target. This gives independent rate-one Yule families. For a single family the forward equations `p_j'=(j-1)p_{j-1}-jp_j`, `p_1(0)=1`, have the solution `p_j=e^{-t}(1-e^{-t})^{j-1}`; direct substitution and successive scalar ODE uniqueness verify this. Hence `E_T n(t)=m e^t` and `E_T n(t)^2 <= m(m+1)e^{2t}`. Consequently `E_T Z=m` by uniform integrability, since `e^{-t}n(t)` is bounded in `L^2`.

**1.4 [INPUT (I1)].** From `P3`, `log C(T_t)/t -> mu` almost surely: the discrete exponent is unchanged by omitting the first jump (the three-vertex starting shape is deterministic) and `log n(t)/t -> 1` by 1.2. Only the almost-sure part of (I1), and `0<mu<1`, will be used below.

## 2. One-leaf influence

**2.1 [PROVED: coupling].** Start from a pair `T,T+x`, identifying the original vertices. Maintain a set of paired vertices whose induced graphs agree exactly; each remaining vertex is called unmatched, even if it could later have been paired by another rule. Use a rate-one clock per pair or unmatched vertex. At a paired source, couple the two uniform neighbour distributions to choose the same paired target `y` with probability `min(1/d_-,1/d_+)` for each paired neighbour `y`: conditional on failing this common part, sample the two residual distributions, normalized by the common failure probability. This produces exactly the two required marginals. The two newborns are paired on common success and are both unmatched on failure. At an unmatched source use its own uniform choice and leave the resulting newborn unmatched. The paired induced graph invariant follows because the only new edges are from the newborns to their targets. Each component has distinct rate-one clocks, and each clock mark viewed in that component is uniform given the joint past. For nonexplosion directly, the total jump rate after `i` joint jumps is at most the initial combined size plus `2i`, since at most two vertices are born per jump. Its holding times can be generated by independent unit exponentials divided by the total rate. Their sum diverges almost surely by the same summable-variance argument as in 1.2. Thus the construction is valid for all time and each component has the required law.

Let `B` be the total number of unmatched vertices in both trees, initially one. At a paired source write `d_0` for the number of paired neighbours and `a,b` for the numbers of unmatched neighbours in the respective trees. Its failure probability is exactly `p=max(a,b)/(d_0+max(a,b))`. The common induced tree initially is all of `T`; each new paired vertex attaches to this tree, so it is connected in both components. Each connected component of unmatched vertices in either tree has at most one edge to the paired subtree: two such edges would give a cycle. Denote the total number of these boundary edges by `J`. Equivalently `J=sum_{paired sources}(a+b)`, and `J<=B`. There is at least one paired neighbour per paired source, so `p<= (a+b)/2`. Therefore, for every joint state reached by the coupling,

```
drift(B) = B + 2 sum p <= 2 B.                                   (2.1)
```

Any paired vertex with disagreeing degrees has an unmatched neighbour; there are at most `J` such vertices. In fact `|N_k^+-N_k^-| <= B` in this coupling: charge each disagreeing pair to an unmatched neighbour on a side where its degree is larger. That neighbour cannot be charged twice, since an unmatched component has at most one boundary edge. The neighbour used for such a charge might itself be counted by `N_k`; to avoid a double charge, use the following finer accounting. For a pair that counts towards the *positive* difference, charge it to an unmatched neighbour on the plus side. If that neighbour is a leaf (`k>=2`), it is not counted by `N_k`. Otherwise charge the pair to any leaf of its unmatched component that is not the endpoint of its unique boundary edge. Such a leaf exists: when the component has at least two vertices, take a vertex farthest from the boundary, which has degree one in the full tree. Distinct pairs charged on this side have distinct components. Thus positive contributions from paired vertices and from unmatched plus vertices together are at most the number of unmatched plus vertices. Apply the same argument to the negative difference. This proves the claimed `B` bound without assuming monotonicity under adding a leaf.

**2.2 [PROVED: retaining the initial degree].** Let `S` be the first failed ring of any pair and put `d=d_T(x)`. Before `S`, all unmatched vertices are leaves of `x` in the plus tree. This is true at the start, and any unmatched ring in this regime targets `x` and creates another such leaf. The absolute difference before `S` is therefore at most the indicator that `d<k` (the paired degree of `x` only increases).

On `s<S`, let `L=B`, the number of those leaves. Before `S` it increases by one at rate `L`; kill this process at `S`. Then `E[L(s);s<S]<=e^s`, for instance by coupling its birth clocks to an autonomous Yule process from one and keeping the latter alive after `S`. The failure intensity on `s<S` is `L/(D_0(x)+L) <= L/(d+1)`. At `S`, `B=L+2` (the pre-jump `L`), and the upper bound from (2.1) for the subsequent mean is `(L+2)e^{2(u-S)}`. Hence the compensation formula for the first failure gives

```
|F_k(u,T+x)-F_k(u,T)|
 <= 1_{d<k}+ (1/(d+1)) int_0^u e^{2(u-s)} E[L(s)(L(s)+2); s<S] ds
 <= 1_{d<k}+ e^{2u}(2u+1-e^{-u})/(d+1).                          (2.3)
```

In the last step use the first two Yule moments: `E[L^2+2L; s<S] <= 2e^{2s}+e^s`. For the general influence take `E B(u)<=e^{2u}` from (2.1), yielding

```
|F_k(u,T+x)-F_k(u,T)| <= e^{2u}.                                  (2.4)
```

For (2.1) integrated in time, localization at the first size cutoff, the compensated jump identity and Gronwall's inequality apply because the stopped jump rates are bounded and `B` is nonnegative. Remove the cutoff by Fatou using nonexplosion. For (2.3) the compensation identity is for a predictable function of the pre-jump pair state at its first failure; the integral is finite by the second Yule moment, so nonnegative truncation and monotone convergence remove the cutoffs. One way to see the compensation identity is to condition on each successive state and holding interval: an event of a given type at time `s` in that interval has probability density its instantaneous rate times the exponentiated negative holding rate. Sum over intervals and use Tonelli for nonnegative functions.

**2.3 [PROVED: summing squared influences].** At `T`, the birth rate at `x` is `q_x=sum_{y~x} 1/d_T(y)`, so `q_x<=d_T(x)`. For `C>=1` the leaves have total `q_x` at most `C`: each can be selected only by its sole neighbour, which is core, and each core has total clock rate one. Among core vertices with `d<k`, the total `q_x` is at most `(k-1)C`. For `d>=k` use (2.3) with zero first term and `q_x/(d+1)^2 <=1`. Thus for all `u<=h`,

```
sum_x q_x |F_k(u,T+x)-F_k(u,T)|^2 <= A_{h,k} C(T),
A_{h,k}=e^{4h}[k+(2h+1-e^{-h})^2].                               (2.5)
```

Without the refinement, (2.4) gives the alternative bound `e^{4h}n(T)` because `sum_x q_x=n(T)`.

## 3. Martingale and concentration

**3.1 [HYP (H); PROVED consequence].** Put `delta=mu-sigma>0`. For `k=2`, (H) gives pointwise `Psi(T)=lim_{u->infty} e^{-mu u}F_2(u,T)` and `E_T C(T_t)<=2K C(T)e^{mu t}`. The semigroup identity yields `E_T Psi(T_t)=e^{mu t}Psi(T)`: for fixed `t`, apply dominated convergence to `e^{-mu u}F_2(u,T_t)`, bounded by `2K C(T_t)` with finite expectation. Thus `M_t=e^{-mu t}Psi(T_t)` is a nonnegative martingale from any tree with core. From the `u=0` bound `Psi` need not be bounded below by a positive multiple of `C`; the proof will not assume that.

**3.2 [STANDARD; PROVED estimate].** For horizon `s+h`, consider its Doob martingale `E[N_k(T_{s+h}) | F_r]=F_k(s+h-r,T_r)` on `[s,s+h]`, where `F_r` is the natural filtration. The terminal variable is square integrable by the second size moment, and the identity is the Markov property from the exponential clock construction. Its mean squared change equals the expectation of its summed squared jumps. To justify the absence of any continuous quadratic variation, between tree jumps the conditional expectation is continuously differentiable in the remaining time. In fact the first-jump decomposition at fixed `T` is

```
F_k(v,T)=e^{-n(T)v}N_k(T)+sum_x q_x int_0^v e^{-n(T)(v-w)} F_k(w,T+x) dw.
```

Here `F_k` is continuous: the process has no jump at a deterministic time, and the size at any slightly later bounded horizon dominates with finite expectation. The displayed identity gives continuous derivatives. On a bounded time interval there are finitely many jumps almost surely, so the continuous pieces contribute no quadratic variation. Apply the square-integrable martingale isometry for quadratic variation, then compensate the sum of squared jumps by the transition rates (the compensation identity described in 2.2). From (2.5) and `E_T C(T_r)<=2K C(T)e^{mu r}`,

```
E_T [N_k(T_{s+h})-F_k(h,T_s)]^2 <= 2K C(T) A_{h,k} h e^{mu(s+h)}.  (3.1)
```

With the rough influence instead, the corresponding upper bound is `e^{4h} h n(T)e^{s+h}`. Normalized by `e^{2mu(s+h)}`, that bound decays for fixed `h>0` just when `mu>1/2`; for the growing horizons below the rough bound suffices if `4beta<2mu-1`. The refined (3.1) decays for fixed `h` for any positive `mu`, and allows the growing horizons below whenever `4beta<mu`. Thus the proof works also for `0<mu<=1/2` given the same almost-sure exponent input and (H) for that `mu`. At `mu=0` the refined bound no longer gives the needed exponential decay, and the small-tail conclusion would in any case fail since `C_n>=1` for `n>=3`.

**3.3 [PROVED].** Use increasing horizons in (3.1) to approximate the observable directly.

Fix `beta>0` small enough that `4beta<mu/2` (e.g. `beta=mu/16`). At terminal time `t>=1`, set `s=(1-beta)t`, `h=beta t`. From (3.1), noting `A_{h,k}<=e^{4h}[k+(2h+1)^2]`,

```
E_T |e^{-mu t}[N_k(T_t)-F_k(h,T_s)]|^2
 <= 2K C(T) h [k+(2h+1)^2] exp[-(mu-4beta)t].                     (3.2)
```

From (H),

```
|e^{-mu t} F_k(h,T_s)-l_k M_s| <= K e^{-delta beta t} e^{-mu s} C(T_s).
```

Its expectation is at most `2K^2 C(T)e^{-delta beta t}`. Choose once and for all `a>0` with `4a<min(mu-4beta,delta beta)`. Markov's inequality with the second moment (3.2) and the last first moment, and Borel–Cantelli on any fixed mesh `t=j/m`, yield

```
e^{-mu t}N_k(T_t)=l_k M_{(1-beta)t}+O(e^{-a t})  almost surely     (3.3)
```

on each such mesh, for each fixed `k`. The implied constant may be taken 2 eventually, with a random onset. Indeed the two exceptional probability bounds are a polynomial in `t` times `exp[-(mu-4beta-2a)t]`, and a constant times `exp[-(delta beta-a)t]`; both series converge. Taking the countable intersection over `m,k` is allowed.

**3.4 [STANDARD].** A nonnegative right-continuous martingale with constant finite expectations converges almost surely to a finite limit (Doob's convergence theorem). Here `M_t` is right-continuous by piecewise constant `T_t` and continuous normalization. Write its limit as `Y_T`. By (3.3) at each mesh, `e^{-mu t}N_k(T_t)->l_k Y_T`. For an arbitrary `t` between consecutive points of mesh `1/m`, the normalized count is squeezed between the mesh values multiplied respectively by `e^{-mu/m}` and `e^{mu/m}`, since `N_k` is nondecreasing. Letting `m` tend to infinity proves all-time convergence, simultaneously over `k`, including when `Y_T=0`.

## 4. A quantitative comparison in first moment; positivity

**4.1 [PROVED].** For each fixed terminal `t`, compare `e^{-mu t}C(T_t)` with `e^{-mu(t+1)}C(T_{t+1})` in `L^1`. Choose the *same* conditioning time `s=(1-beta)t`. By (3.1) and Cauchy–Schwarz, the two normalized Doob fluctuations are bounded in `L^1`, in sum, by `b_1 sqrt(C(T))(1+t)^2 exp[-(mu/2-2beta)t]` with `b_1=(1+e^2)sqrt(22K)`: the horizons after `s` are `h=beta t` and `h=beta t+1`, both at most `1+t`, so `2+(2h+1)^2<=11(1+t)^2`; the factor `e^{2h}` in the standard deviation costs at most `e^2` for the second variable. Each normalized conditional mean differs from `M_s` in `L^1` by at most `2K^2 C(T)e^{-delta beta t}`. Thus for `t>=1`,

```
E_T |e^{-mu(t+1)} C(T_{t+1})-e^{-mu t}C(T_t)| <= b C(T) e^{-2a t}. (4.1)
```

Here `sqrt(C(T))<=C(T)`. An admissible constant is `b=4K^2+b_1 sup_{r>=1} (1+r)^2 exp[-(mu/2-2beta-2a)r]`, which is finite since `2a<mu/2-2beta`; also `2a<delta beta`. By Fatou applied to telescoped absolute increments at `t,t+1,...`, using 3.4, this gives

```
E_T |Y_T-e^{-mu t}C(T_t)| <= b C(T)e^{-2a t}/(1-e^{-2a}).          (4.2)
```

In particular the convergence holds in `L^1` for every initial tree with a core, and (H) implies `E_T Y_T=Psi(T)`.

**4.2 [PROVED using STANDARD Borel–Cantelli and INPUT (I1)].** At integer `t`, (4.2) and Markov's inequality bound the probability of an error larger than `e^{-a t}` by `b C(T)e^{-a t}/(1-e^{-2a})`, a summable series. On `Y_{P3}=0`, eventually `C(T_t)<=e^{(mu-a)t}` along integer times. This contradicts 1.4 on its probability-one event. Thus `Y_{P3}>0` almost surely. Finiteness was obtained in 3.4. The clock conversion is now direct: at size `j`,

```
C(T_{tau_j}) / j^mu = [e^{-mu tau_j} C(T_{tau_j})]/[j e^{-tau_j}]^mu
                    -> X_T := Y_T/Z^mu.                          (4.3)
```

For the original continuous process from the edge, let `theta` be its first holding time, finite almost surely. After `theta` the future has the `P3` law, by the restart construction 1.1. Its normalized counts therefore have the all-time limits of 3.4 multiplied by `e^{-mu theta}`, and the original size has limit `e^{-theta} Z` after its own normalization. These factors cancel in (4.3). Thus the ratio limit from the edge is `X=X_{P3}` in law, which proves (a). The convergence and identity (4.3) also hold from every finite deterministic initial tree with a core, without asserting (I1) for those starts.

## 5. The two tails

**5.1 [PROVED: a useful clock-moment identity].** From a fixed tree of size `m`, the variables `Z` and `X_T` in (4.3) are independent: by 1.1 the holding times and the entire jump chain are independent (iterate the conditional transition law), `Z` is a function of the first, and the a.s. limit of the discrete normalized counts is a function of the second. Tonelli and 4.1 give

```
E_T X_T = Psi(T)/E_m Z^mu.                                       (5.1)
```

For all `m>=2`, `p_m=P(Z>=m/2)>=1/6`: `E Z=m`, `E Z^2<=m(m+1)` by Fatou and 1.3, and `m/2 <= E[Z;Z>=m/2] <= sqrt(E Z^2 * p_m)` gives `p_m>=m/[4(m+1)]>=1/6`. Consequently

```
(m/2)^mu/6 <= E_m Z^mu <= m^mu,                                  (5.2)
```

with the upper bound by Jensen's inequality for the concave power `0<mu<1`.

**5.2 [PROVED: small values].** Force the first birth from the edge to attach at a specified endpoint (probability `1/2`). At each subsequent step through size `m`, require a specified original adjacent leaf to ring. It remains a leaf, so the target is always the same center, and the required probability is `(1/2) prod_{j=3}^{m-1}1/j>0`. Conditional on this prescribed discrete history, the future from size `m` starts at the star `S_m` and its limit has the law of `X_{S_m}` by the Markov property. Since `C(S_m)=1`, `Psi(S_m)<=K`; (5.1)–(5.2) give `E X_{S_m}<=6 K (2/m)^mu`. Choose `m` with this bound less than `eps/2`. Markov gives conditional probability at least `1/2` for `X<eps`, proving the first tail.

**5.3 [PROVED: a reference root can grow fast].** Run a free reference process from `P3`, with center `v`. On a positive-probability event there are constants `c>0` and `t_0<infinity` (possibly random) such that `D_v(t)>=c e^{t/2}` for `t>=t_0`. Here is a construction. Track a subset of leaf neighbours of `v`, initially the two leaves. Each tracked leaf rings at rate one, attaches a new leaf at `v`, and the new leaf is tracked. Each tracked leaf is promoted at rate `1/D_v<=1/2`, and is removed from tracking upon promotion. Add a dummy removal at rate `1/2-1/D_v` for each currently tracked leaf (removal from tracking alone, not from the tree). Only a ring at `v` can promote a tracked leaf. The joint construction has the original tree's transition rates, and the tracked population `L` has jump rates `L` up and `L/2` down given the entire past. These rates determine its pure birth-death law by the holding-time construction: while `L` is fixed the two total rates are fixed even through other jumps; equivalently split its two rates as independent per-individual birth and death clocks, use the indicated state-dependent mark to distinguish actual promotions from dummy ones, and drive the remaining tree transitions at their residual rates. Nonexplosion follows from the nonexplosive tree law and the fact that dummy removals only remove tracked vertices, with at most two plus the number of births to remove.

For completeness, `W(t)=e^{-t/2}L(t)` is an `L^2`-bounded nonnegative martingale with mean 2. The drift of `L` is `L/2` and that of `L^2` is `L^2+(3/2)L`. The resulting moment equations are justified by domination on each finite interval by the tree's size at its end, which has finite second moment, and compensation with cutoff (the absolute compensated drifts are bounded by a constant times size squared). They give `E L(t)=2e^{t/2}` and `E W(t)^2 = 4+6(1-e^{-t/2})`. The conditional version of the first drift equation, starting at any deterministic time with the given tracked population, shows the martingale property. Doob's `L^2` convergence theorem gives a limit of mean 2, hence positive with positive probability. This gives the asserted degree growth since `D_v>=L`.

Let `A_t` be the product of `D_v(r-)/(D_v(r-)+1)` over reference rings of `v` before or at `t`, and `A_infty=lim_t A_t`. On the degree growth event, the sum of `1/D_v(r-)` over those rings is finite. Indeed, for each integer `j` the Poisson clock has a rate-one expected number of points on `[j,j+1)`; `sum_j e^{-j/2} #(rings on [j,j+1))` has finite expectation and is finite almost surely, regardless of the degree growth event. The finitely many initial rings are harmless. Since `log(1+1/D)<=1/D`, this implies `A_infty>0` with positive probability. By 4.2, `Y_{P3}>0` almost surely; no independence from `A_infty` is needed. Fatou now gives

```
liminf_{t->infty} e^{-mu t} E_{P3}[C(T_t) A_t] >= E_{P3}[Y_{P3} A_infty] > 0. (5.3)
```

The expectation on the right is finite by 4.1. Fatou may be applied along any sequence of times tending to infinity, since 3.4 is an all-time limit.

**5.4 [PROVED: many copies and a large-value early history].** For integer `b>=2` let `G_b` consist of a hub with `b` neighbours, each the center of a separate `P3` branch. It has `m=3b+1` vertices and `C=b+1`. For one specified branch, compare its far side (the component after deleting its edge to the hub) to a free copy of `P3` as follows. Share all clocks and choices inside that branch with the reference until separation, except that at its center a fresh independent uniform mark accepts the reference choice with probability `D/(D+1)` and otherwise chooses the hub, where `D` is the reference degree just before the ring. At the hub rank the specified center first using a separate uniform mark to choose among all hub neighbours. Separation occurs at a rejection or a hub ring choosing that center; process the separating ring according to the just-stated choices, then continue with ordinary marginal laws and independent choices as needed. Fix the reference clock sources and choice coordinates independently of the exterior clock sources and of all acceptance marks; assign shared clock sources to paired newborns before separation and otherwise use fresh sources in the actual process. The actual root's choice is uniform among its `D+1` neighbours before separation, hub choice is uniform, and all other choices have the required laws. No other exterior source can attach into the branch, because its boundary edge is the only edge to the exterior. Thus on no separation the branch equals the reference, with the additional boundary edge at the center; its core count in the actual tree is at least the reference count.

The hub has degree at least `b`, so no hub mark at most `1/b` through time `t` suffices for no hub entry; this event has probability `e^{-t/b}` by Poisson thinning and is independent of the reference and its acceptance marks. Given the reference, the probability of accepting every root choice is `A_t`. The actual tree therefore has expected core count within the specified branch at least `e^{-t/b} E_{P3}[C(T_t) A_t]`. Apply this separately to each branch and sum the expectations. These are estimates of marginals of the actual tree, so the couplings for different branches need not be run jointly. The far sides remain disjoint, since newborns join the far side containing their attachment target, or none. Consequently

```
Psi(G_b) >= b e^{-t/b} e^{-mu t} E_{P3}[C(T_t) A_t] - K(b+1)e^{-delta t}. (5.4)
```

By (5.3) choose a constant `g>0` and then one finite `t` so large that `e^{-mu t} E_{P3}[C(T_t)A_t]>=g` and `K e^{-delta t}<=g/8`. For all sufficiently large `b`, `e^{-t/b}>=1/2` and `b+1<=2b`; hence `Psi(G_b)>=gb/4`. By (5.1)–(5.2), `E X_{G_b}>=gb/[4(3b+1)^mu]`, unbounded as `b` increases because `mu<1`. For any finite `M`, choose `b` for which this mean exceeds `M`; then `P(X_{G_b}>M)>0`.

To realize `G_b`, force attachments at one endpoint of the initial edge until that endpoint is the center of the star with `b` leaves (for `b=2` the first birth suffices). Then in any specified order attach two leaves at each of those original star leaves, by requiring the hub to ring and choose that neighbour at each of the `2b` steps. The hub's degree in this phase is exactly `b`, so the conditional probabilities are `1/(jb)` at size `j`; their product times the initial star-history probability is positive. Restarting at that deterministic size with this tree gives the future law used above. This proves the upper tail for `X` from the edge.

## 6. Degree limits

**6.1 [PROVED].** On the common probability-one event of 3.4 and 4.2, divide `e^{-mu t}N_k(T_t)` by `e^{-mu t}C(T_t)`, whose limit from `P3` is strictly positive. The limit is `l_k` for every `k>=2`. Evaluate along the size hitting times and subtract adjacent cumulative counts to get `N_{=k}/C -> l_k-l_{k+1}` simultaneously for all `k`.

## Inputs used

- **INPUT (I1):** the discrete almost-sure core exponent from the edge (hence from `P3`) and `0<mu<1`. No use of the mean exponent theorem apart from the value of `mu` in the hypothesis.
- **STANDARD (I2):** independent-sum convergence, exponential memorylessness and the Markov property of the clock chain, the Doob martingale of a square-integrable variable and the martingale isometry, Doob's nonnegative martingale convergence theorem, Fatou, dominated convergence, Jensen, Cauchy–Schwarz, Markov and Borel–Cantelli. Their integrability and measurability conditions are checked where applied.
- **HYP (H):** detailed below.

## Exactly which form of (H) is used where

- Small fixed horizons are not essential: an estimate for all sufficiently large horizons with uniform threshold suffices. The bound on `Psi` follows from the core error estimate at any fixed admissible horizon and `E_T C(T_v)<=C(T)e^v`: the promotion rate is at most `C(T)` at any state with a core (all promotions are driven by core vertices). This last expectation bound follows by compensation, stopping, and Gronwall as in 2.2. Neither a positive lower bound on `Psi/C` nor positivity of the individual `l_k` is used (the limits `l_k` must be the same for the different starting states being compared).
- **Higher degrees need no gap under the core weight.** Run the proof first for `k=2`, retaining its exponential error. For each `k>=3`, it suffices that `|e^{-mu u}F_k(u,T)-l_k Psi(T)| <= C(T) r_k(u)` with a deterministic `r_k(u)->0`. In (3.3) the concentration error is still exponentially small along the meshes, and the normalized mean error is bounded by `r_k(beta t) e^{-mu s}C(T_s)`, which tends to zero almost surely since the second factor has already been shown to converge. In particular there is no need for uniformity in `k`. For the enlarged weight variants below, the stated exponential gap and its indicated inequalities are sufficient for all `k` (these gaps and constants may depend on `k`).
- **A larger weight genuinely suffices.** Replace `C(T)` in both analytic bounds by `C(T)^p`, with the same `delta>0` and any `p>1` satisfying `(p-1)(2-mu)<delta beta/(1-beta)`, where `0<beta<mu/4`. For a fixed starting tree `T` the mean upper bound in 3.1 becomes `2K C(T)^p e^{mu t}`. Semigroup domination uses `E C(T_t)^p<infinity`, which holds since at a fixed time the Yule size has all moments. In 3.2 the variance rate is unchanged, and only its prefactor `C(T)` changes to `C(T)^p`. Choose `p<=2`; by Hölder, `E C(T_s)^p <= (E C(T_s))^{2-p}(E n(T_s)^2)^{p-1}`, bounded by a constant depending on the starting tree times `exp[(mu+(p-1)(2-mu))s]`. In 3.3 and 4.1 the expected normalized error then decays at rate `delta beta-(p-1)(2-mu)(1-beta)>0`. This still yields exponential convergence on the mesh and `E Y_T=Psi(T)` from every fixed start; the prefactors need not be uniform in `T`. Stars still have `Psi(S_m)<=K`. In (5.4), take `t=L log b` with `L>(p-1)/delta`; then the enlarged error `K(b+1)^p e^{-delta t}` is `o(b)` while the hub factor tends to one. Thus both tails still hold. If a separate `Psi` bound is omitted under `C^p`, the fixed-horizon argument in the preceding bullet supplies it with that same weight.
- **Pure size weight.** Simply replacing `C(T)` by `n(T)` at the same unspecified gap does not close this argument. The normalized conditioning error is now bounded in mean by a constant times `exp[-(delta beta-(1-mu)(1-beta))t]`, and the star bound on `Psi` is lost. A sufficient size-weight variant is `delta> (1-mu)(4/mu-1)` (equivalently there is a `beta<mu/4` for which that error rate is positive). The fixed-start mean bound and concentration still work with this change and prefactors depending on the start. For stars the mean promotion bound and size-weight error give `Psi(S_m)<=e^{(1-mu)u}+Km e^{-delta u}`. Set `u=(log m)/(1-mu+delta)`: the resulting bound is `(1+K)m^{(1-mu)/(1-mu+delta)}`, which is `o(m^mu)` under the stated stronger gap. For the large tail the trees `G_b` have size `3b+1`, so a fixed sufficiently large `t` in (5.4) works with error bounded by `K(3b+1)e^{-delta t}`. These conditions are sufficient for the proof; no necessity or impossibility for the unrestricted size-weight variant is asserted.

## Gaps

None in the implication from (H). The analytic hypothesis itself is not proved here.

## Exact small-case checks

**[PROVED finite checks].** `tests.py` enumerates all 47 unlabelled trees of sizes 2 through 8 by adjoining one leaf in every position and removing duplicates via a canonical rooted-bracket encoding minimized over roots. Completeness follows by deleting a leaf from any tree of the next size. For each initial tree and attachment vertex, the program uses the exact generator rates and the independence of jump chain and holding times to evaluate the terms for zero through six jumps. At `q=e^{-u}`, the mass for `j` jumps from size `n` is `binom(n+j-1,j) q^n (1-q)^j`, by the sum of geometric Yule populations in 1.3. The exact omitted size first moment bounds the omitted expectation of each count above; zero bounds it below. The subtraction and absolute value use interval bounds, and every comparison with an influence upper bound uses a rational lower bound on its right-hand side (expand `-log q` in nonnegative powers of `1-q`). Thus passing comparisons are certified, including at `u=0`. The tests also enumerate local coupling geometry and degree triples. The log records two intentionally failing inequalities: drift bounded by `1.5 B`, and zero influence on already-core vertices. These checks illustrate the estimates and do not replace the general proof.

## Where the proof would break

1. A uniform bound on influence with only a size rate would require `2mu>1`; it is crucial for the stated refinement that a divergent pair-ring does not cause any new discrepancies before its first occurrence, and that before then the unmatched vertices are all leaves at the attachment target.
2. Mean convergence or a nonnegative martingale alone does not imply almost-sure positivity. The exponential rate in (4.2) is what rules out convergence to zero on a set with the prescribed almost-sure exponent.
3. A support argument must control the normalized limit for an entire continuation after the prescribed history. Simply observing a large value at one early size supplies no such lower bound.
