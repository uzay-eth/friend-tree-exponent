## 1. Gut reaction in one minute

The pleasing part is not that a guess lost. It’s that Paul Krapivsky and Sid Redner got the exponent right to three decimal places from a rule you can explain in a sentence. The certified interval contains numbers that round to 0.566, but not 17/30. Those are compatible facts, not competing verdicts. And then there’s the result I didn’t expect: individual trees retain a random multiplier, yet their non-leaves settle into the same degree proportions. That’s the story I’d tell. The PDF has a better title than the README. The abstract starts well and then turns into a catalogue before I’ve had time to wonder why the nucleus grows so slowly.

## 2. What I would do next

Draw the three-vertex path. If either leaf is picked, the center gains a leaf and the nucleus stays put; if the center is picked, a leaf becomes a non-leaf. So the nucleus grows at the next step with probability 1/3. On a general tree the rate in the rate-one clocks is the sum of the leaf-neighbor fractions over core vertices. I’d use that to explain why counting non-leaves alone doesn’t give a closed recursion: two cores of the same size can have very different fractions. Then I’d put the old numerical result, the exact upper bound that rules out 17/30, and the tighter interval on one number line. I’d send that short explanation and the proof of the degree law to a human probabilist and to Krapivsky and Redner for a check of both the mathematics and the history. I wouldn’t describe this cold read or the model replays as human peer review.

## 3. Sentences I marked

### Supplied PDF

- **Abstract:** “This rules out 17/30 and agrees with Krapivsky and Redner’s estimate of 0.566 to the reported precision.” True if “reported precision” means rounding to three decimal places. But §5.1 of their paper also gives a finite-fit range of 0.5652–0.5673 and says the estimate seems accurate to within 0.001. Say “rounds to their estimate of 0.566.” The fit range does not itself discriminate between 17/30 and the new interval.
- **Abstract:** “For each fixed degree $k\ge2$, the fraction of non-leaves of degree $k$ converges almost surely to a deterministic limit of order $k^{-(1+\mu)}$ as $k$ grows, as Krapivsky and Redner conjectured.” The two limits run into one another, and the attribution is too direct. Krapivsky–Redner §5.1, equations (32)–(33), discusses mean counts and the normalized degree tail. Addario-Berry et al. §7(2) states the almost-sure, fixed-$k$ formulation and credits them. First take the size limit, then take $k$ large; credit the prediction and its later formulation separately.
- **Abstract:** “The limiting degrees divided by $n$ sum to one almost surely.” The division comes *before* the limit. The full sum of normalized degrees at size $n$ tends to two, not one. The sum of the fixed-label limits is one. That distinction is the interesting part of Theorem 9.
- **§1:** “Krapivsky and Redner studied this growth rule as isotropic redirection.” True, but as the opening of the historical account it leaves the rule’s origin unstated. The cited *Random friend trees* explicitly credits Saramäki and Kaski with introducing the undirected rule. Credit them for the rule and Krapivsky and Redner for studying it as isotropic redirection and identifying the nucleus scaling.
- **§1:** “In 2022 Strogatz brought their question to a wider audience and asked whether the exponent could be 17/30 [3].” The post says Redner told Strogatz about the work with Krapivsky, then poses the exact-value question. It doesn’t establish who first thought of 17/30. “Their question” can be read as assigning that particular rational guess to Krapivsky and Redner. State what the post establishes, including Redner’s role in the conversation.
- **§2:** “The finite-size prefactors and an independent degree-band upper bound 5657/9983 appear in the original paper, Theorem 1.2.” “Original paper” sounds like [2] or [4] here. The link is to Uzay’s earlier write-up, where the product bounds and the separate degree-band certificate actually appear. Name that write-up.
- **§2:** “Beyond a degree cutoff an endpoint is a mixture of a finite corner and an infinite-degree corner with mean reciprocal degree exactly $1/D_v$.” “An endpoint” sounds like a random vertex in the model. It’s the lookup for a motif endpoint that is interpolated, not the tree or its degrees. The linked proof gives the simple weights $d_j/D_v$ and $1-d_j/D_v$ and explains why matching the reciprocal alone isn’t the whole drift check.
- **§3, degree-ratio conjecture:** “Krapivsky and Redner’s prediction [2] in this notation is” (introducing the displayed almost-sure limit). Same attribution problem as in the abstract. [2] is the physical prediction; [4, §7(2)] is the source for the displayed almost-sure formulation.
- **Theorem 6:** “Assume the finite-exterior planting and one-edge hypotheses of Lemma B.4.” The scope is broader than needed where it sits: the next sentence, “There is a Borel probability $\pi$ with $\pi Q_t=e^{\mu t}\pi$ for all $t\ge0$,” is already established by B.3 without those hypotheses. The conditional part is the spectral comparison on the stated space and the exclusion of other probability eigenvalues. The supplied README makes that distinction more clearly.
- **Theorem 9 proof:** “For the edge seed one can use the external growth input $\mathbb E C_n=O(n^\beta)$ for some $\beta<1$, from Theorem 1 (only the sublinear mean bound is used); Tonelli then gives summability almost surely.” “External” means external to the summability argument, not external to this paper; Theorem 1 is right here.
- **Theorem 13 proof:** “The count and uniform log-volume CDF are the lower-tier premises, not conclusions of this theorem; see the count/CDF inputs and support-size proof.” The distinction is right. “Lower-tier” is editorial vocabulary the reader hasn’t yet been given and doesn’t say which result proved the premises, or under what hypotheses. State that they’re separate inputs with narrower review. Read (3) as $V\le N^x$, not $V\le Nx$.
- **§5:** “The spatial proof gives $-\log P(X>x)\sim Ix^{1/(1-\mu)}$ as $x\to\infty$.” In this paragraph the left tail’s assumptions are explicit, while the right tail sounds like a consequence of the main theorems. Identify the right-tail input assumptions and link the proof of the asymptotic equivalent directly. The linked `open/limit-law` note gives matching upper and lower stretched-exponential bounds, which alone don’t give a single limiting rate constant. A review tier is not a hypothesis list.
- **§5:** “The shape law converges in total variation to the age mixture for $N\le c_0\log n$, with $c_0$ given in terms of a spectral gap, not numerically certified.” On a standalone reading this includes fixed cutoffs. Carry forward the condition $N\to\infty$ and the stated external estimates into this sentence; also repeat that it’s the law that counts components once, not the surviving-core sampling law.
- **§6:** “The fixed-degree law and intermediate cumulative law do not settle the pointwise growing-degree law of Krapivsky–Redner [2] throughout the intermediate range.” Good distinction, but “intermediate cumulative law” hasn’t been stated in the supplied PDF, and neither range is identified here. Give the earlier cumulative result a reference and spell out the still-open joint limit for the expected *exact* degree count. This is not the fixed-degree conjecture just answered.
- **Appendix C.2:** “At $d\ge32$ direct excess is zero and $U(d+1)<L(2)$ by the validated cap $u=1/(33p_{\mathrm{lo}})$, where $h_{\mathrm{lo}}$ is the box lower endpoint.” The formula says $p_{\mathrm{lo}}$ while the prose defines $h_{\mathrm{lo}}$. Use one symbol, especially in a paragraph meant to be compared to interval code.

### Supplied README

- **Results:** “For fixed $k\ge2$ the mean number of vertices of degree at least $k$ is asymptotic to $c_k n^\mu$, and the fraction of non-leaves of degree exactly $k$ converges almost surely to $p_k=(c_k-c_{k+1})/c_2$ with $p_k\sim a k^{-1-\mu}$ for some $a>0$, answering the Krapivsky–Redner conjecture recorded in [*Random friend trees*, §7(2)](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.20185v1#S7).” The link helps, but attach “answering” specifically to the fixed-degree ratio and its subsequent tail, not the whole compound claim or the growing-degree prediction. Also, “tied founders” means their shares agree **in law**; it doesn’t mean they get equal shares in a given tree.

Publicizing a question is not the same contribution as finding the exponent numerically or originating the exact guess.

## 4. Which theorem I’d check first, and can I?

**Theorem 1, beginning with just its upper bound.** To rule out 17/30 I don’t need Gold2, the amplitude law, or even the lower certificate. I need a potential comparable to the core count with one-step expected drift at most $(1133/2000)\Phi/n$. Iterate the product; the gap to 17/30 is exactly $1/6000$. A mean bound alone also gives the almost-sure *upper* logarithmic bound by Markov on dyadic sizes and monotonicity. It doesn’t prove existence, the matching lower bound, or equality of the exponents. For those I’d go back to Appendix A, especially the separation of marginal site bounds from the independence of the retained experiments in Lemma A.3.

## 5. One edit

Replace the opening of “The questions behind the numbers” with one paragraph that gets the credit and the stakes right before introducing the other theorems:

> Saramäki and Kaski introduced this one-step attachment rule. Studying it as isotropic redirection, Paul Krapivsky and Sid Redner called the non-leaves the nucleus and estimated that its mean size grows with exponent about 0.566. After hearing about their work from Redner, Steven Strogatz asked in 2022 whether the exponent might be exactly 17/30. Theorem 1 rules that out; Theorem 2 narrows the interval to values that all round to 0.566. The numerical estimate stands. What the exact bounds exclude is the proposed fraction.
