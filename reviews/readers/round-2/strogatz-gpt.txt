## 1. Gut reaction in one minute

The nicest news is that Paul Krapivsky and Sid Redner had the exponent right to three decimal places, and the tempting fraction is nevertheless wrong. That is a better story than “a conjecture was disproved.” Their estimate was for average growth; finding the same exponent in an individual tree, with a random multiplier that never washes out, is the bigger surprise. I would keep reading for the mechanism. The paper asks a lovely simple question, but it hands me a catalogue of answers before showing me why counting non-leaves is hard.

## 2. What I would do next

I would put Krapivsky and Redner’s §5.1 beside §7(2) of *Random friend trees* and then read Theorems 4–5. The 2017 paper gives the numerical evidence for the nucleus and fixed-degree **mean** counts and the power-law prediction for their normalized counts. Addario-Berry and colleagues state the almost-sure version. Here the limit in tree size comes first, at each fixed degree, and only then does the limiting degree distribution get a power-law tail. I’d follow the new argument from the exponential age mixture to the cumulative tail and then to the crossing flux. That last step is worth explaining: you can’t just subtract two tail equivalents and call the difference an equivalent for the point probability.

## 3. Sentences I would flag

**Actual overclaims or wrong attribution: none found in the headline claims on this reading.** The 2017 paper really does report 0.566 and the stated range of slopes; the 2022 post explicitly credits Redner and Krapivsky and proposes 17/30 as the exact value. The later 0.569 is accurately called a fit, not a competing theorem. The packet credits the earlier walk rule to Saramäki and Kaski, the rigorous friend-tree groundwork and questions to Addario-Berry and colleagues, and the earlier generation-sum characterization, effective approximation and constant-factor mean bounds to Long. It distinguishes Long’s operator from the marked-fringe operator here and credits Sabba’s reported numbers without using them as certificate inputs. The moment order is not sold as stochastic order. Nor is a replay sold as an independent full implementation or human refereeing. These are reading judgments, not a proof audit or an independent priority search.

**Clarity, not changes to the claims:**

- PDF, abstract: “This proves the fixed-degree formulation of Krapivsky and Redner’s scaling prediction in Section 7(2) of Addario-Berry et al.” I momentarily read this as a claim that Krapivsky and Redner themselves stated the almost-sure formulation. The sentence is defensible, and the body gets the distinction right. The abstract would be clearer if the two groups each had their own verb: one predicted the scaling, the other formulated the almost-sure question.
- PDF, §1: “The vertices of degree at least two form a smaller tree, the core.” Not at the starting edge: the core is empty until the first birth. This is a missing qualifier in the introduction, not a problem with the theorem.
- PDF, Theorem 2 proof: “The new singleton is charged to its promoter.” “Removing the newborn from any newly counted larger configuration identifies a supported old configuration.” On first pass I took “newborn” to mean the new **leaf**. It means the old leaf that just became a core vertex; the removal argument concerns newly created **supported** motifs. Both matter when translating the growth rule into the bookkeeping.
- PDF, abstract: “The two founders have the same law.” Their limiting **shares** have the same marginal distribution; the shares need not be equal on the same tree. Section 4.2 says exactly that. The same shorthand appears in §1: “The two founders tie in law, and the chance of a positive share decreases strictly from label two onward (Theorem 10).” And in the README: “The founders tie in law; from label 2 onward, the chance of a positive share and every positive moment strictly decrease with birth label.” The qualifier “from label 2 onward” is correct: the first strict comparison is 2 versus 3, not 1 versus 2.

## 4. Which theorem I would check first, and whether the paper lets me

Theorem 1, upper side first. I don’t need the five-vertex calculation, or even existence of the limiting exponent, to see whether the old fraction survives. The three-vertex drift bound for a positive potential comparable to the core count gives a factor of \(1+(1133/2000)/n\) per step in expectation. The product gives an upper power, and Markov plus Borel–Cantelli on dyadic sizes, with monotonicity between them, gives the almost-sure upper exponent. The exact gap is \(17/30-1133/2000=1/6000\). Appendix A is where I would turn next for existence and equality of the two exponents, especially the distinction between independent comparison variables and actual branches sharing an exterior.

## 5. One edit

Put a two-tree example immediately after the opening question, before the theorem list: “Take two adjacent non-leaves and hang four leaves off them. Split the leaves 1 and 3, and the rate of making a new non-leaf is \(1/2+3/4=5/4\). Split them 2 and 2, and it is \(2/3+2/3=4/3\). The trees have the same size and the same nucleus size, but the nucleus grows at different rates.” Now I know what Krapivsky and Redner were measuring and why counting the nucleus alone won’t prove their exponent. The weighted configurations have a reason to exist before I am asked to trust millions of checks.
