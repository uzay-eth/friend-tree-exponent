## 1. Gut reaction in one minute

I would read this for the almost-sure normalization and the conservation of hub mass, not for the last decimal of the exponent. Showing that \(\log C_n/\log n\to\mu\) and showing that \(C_n/n^\mu\) has a finite, strictly positive, genuinely random limit are different results. The paper appears to know exactly where that difference costs work. The hub-mass theorem is the other result I would tell a colleague about: every fixed-label limit can exist and yet probability can escape to labels tending to infinity. Showing that it does not is the point. The numerical headline is a good hook, but not the best account of what is new here.

Then I would verify Theorem 9 on paper. At size \(n\), \(q_n(v)=L_v(n)/n\) on cores has total mass \(1-C_n/n\); the difference \(P_n-q_n\) is the contribution of core initiators and has mass \(C_n/n\). Neither vector is the degree vector of total mass \(2-2/n\). Coordinatewise convergence gives at most unit limiting mass. The proof that gives *at least* unit mass is the late-promotion charge: a newly promoted vertex costs \(2/r\), and the normalized degrees of the vertices already charged have nonpositive drift. The stop includes the crossing step and freezes the denominator as well as the labels. Summation by parts bounds the expected stopped tail by a summable tail of the frozen count; Fatou and then removal of the stop rule out escape. I could follow this without importing the new exponent: the old Proposition 6.22 is sufficient for summability. That independence is worth preserving. Once unit mass is known, the passage to label TV is straightforward; weak convergence on normalized degrees also uses the moving atom locations, so is a separate step.

## 3. Sentences I would mark

**Substantive proof wording, not a counterexample to the conclusions**

- “For the first enclosure, the three-vertex proof constructs two potentials comparable above and below to \(C_n\) by fixed positive factors.” The linked comparison actually certifies the needed sides separately: \(\Phi_+\ge c_0 C\) and \(\Phi_-\le C_0 C\), with positive values at \(T_3\). It explicitly does not need positivity throughout for the lower witness. If the sentence means *each* potential is comparable in both directions, that is stronger than what the cited comparison establishes. The stated bracket follows from the one-sided bounds.
- “Now fix \(v\) and start at its promotion time \(\tau\) (time zero for a founder).” Together with “When that ring promotes a leaf, the new core follows its attached copy.” This is not literally a physical coupling at the edge start. If founder \(v\) rings first, it promotes the other founder, whose two neighbours are then leaves; the copied newborn has a core exterior parent and one leaf. Use the construction once \(v\) is a core, also for a founder (start no earlier than size three). This is a finite-prefix repair; the infinitely-many-children argument does not need those first rings.

**Attribution and clarity, not overclaims of the results**

- “This proves the fixed-degree formulation of Krapivsky and Redner’s scaling prediction in Section 7(2) of Addario-Berry et al.” The modifier “in Section 7(2)” can attach to “scaling prediction” rather than “formulation.” In Krapivsky–Redner §5.1, (32)–(33) concern mean counts and a nucleus-normalized degree law; Addario-Berry et al. §7(2) states the almost-sure fixed-degree formulation, crediting that work. The body gets the distinction right. The README sentence beginning “Krapivsky and Redner predicted the degree law” also gets it right. I would not call this a wrong attribution once read in context.
- “This answers Questions 1, 2, 4 and the first part of Question 3 of Section 7.” Correct collectively, but the immediate antecedent is the birth-order sentence. Besides making the referent ambiguous, the compression hides that Question 2 has two preliminary asks, both answered. It is not a claim to settle all of §7.
- “The two founders have the same law.” This is about their limiting shares, not equality of shares on one tree. Theorem 10 and §4.2 make that clear; the abstract could name the variables in the sentence.
- “A silent planted newborn is a core with one leaf and one exterior slot that counts in its degree, sends nothing and whose incoming arrows are null; all other rates are physical.” The relative clause is hard to parse. The slot sends nothing **into** the branch, and a root choice **of** the slot is null for the intrinsic chain. Internal arrows are physical. The separate outgoing channel in the coupling makes the intended reading recoverable.
- “In particular both distances tend to one when \(\alpha=1\), for example at \(N=\lfloor n/\log n\rfloor\).” Correct under (3); a reader quoting just that sentence could mistake the example for an unconditional assertion of Theorem 13. The theorem opens with the assumptions and the proof explicitly repeats that they are premises. I would keep the qualification attached to the example when citing it.

No other substantive overclaim or wrong attribution found on this read. In particular, the founder-hub citation is the founder-edge case of Addario-Berry et al. v1 Theorem 3.2, not their Theorem 3.1 on fixed-label limits; their §3 conjecture is about a conditional law, as claimed. The roles of the older Krapivsky–Redner estimate, the Strogatz question, and the later 0.569 fit are distinguished. The pinned Long sources support credit for the earlier inert-newborn characterization, effective two-sided approximation and constant-factor mean bounds; neither a new marked-fringe kernel nor a pathwise amplitude should be described as the first characterization of the exponent. I did not independently adjudicate the supplementary proof or review histories.

## 4. Theorem I would check first, and whether I can

**Theorem 4.** Yes: the PDF gives enough to start a real proof check, including the analytic input in Lemmas B.1–B.2 and the probabilistic input in Appendix A. My first question is whether the all-seed mean error is genuinely \(O(C(T)e^{\sigma t})\), with \(\sigma<\mu\), after summing the residual estimates over overlapping fringes and integrating founder immigration. A mean asymptotic only from the edge will not survive conditioning at a large random tree. The paper gives the generator cancellation (not independence of physical fringes), a summable ancestral weight, and a finite matrix on **marks**, not a truncation of physical degrees. I can locate the nonlattice step and see why subtracting the one-generation term makes contour inversion absolutely integrable.

The next check is that a single added leaf, including one that promotes its target, creates at most three initial dirty cores; only dirty core rings spread the discrepancy to new pairs. This is why the conditional-variance error involves size rather than size squared. The use of \(2\mu>1\) and a logarithmic lag is visible in the summability estimate. The nonnegative martingale's positive expectation would not prove strict positivity almost surely: the proof instead combines a conditional second-moment estimate with \(|T|/\Psi(T)^2\to0\) from the logarithmic path exponent and uses convergence of the conditional probabilities to the event indicator. Finally the fixed meshes are intersected countably; only the cumulative counts are interpolated by monotonicity. At size epochs the factor is \(W_{\rm size}^{-\mu}\), not an independent factor to be pulled out of an expectation involving \(M_\infty\). The discrete martingale is used for the mean transfer. These are all checkable steps in the packet, not a substitute for a line-by-line verification of the couplings and the uniform remainder. The sharp Gold2 interval, the conditional part of Theorem 6, and the density proof are not prerequisites.
